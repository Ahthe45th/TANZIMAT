- There are a few reliable ways to spot whether an image was generated by AI without relying only on “how it looks.” None are perfect alone, but together they make it much easier.
- 
- Here are the strongest methods:
- 
- ---
- 
- ### **1. Check the metadata (EXIF)**
- 
- Real photos often contain:
- 
- * Camera model
- * Lens type
- * ISO, shutter speed
- * GPS (sometimes)
- * Editing history
- 
- AI-generated images usually have:
- 
- * **Missing EXIF**
- * **Blank camera info**
- * Odd generator tags (e.g., “StableDiffusion,” “AIImage,” etc.)
- 
- You can check using:
- 
- * exiftool (Linux)
- * metadata2go.com
- * Jeffrey’s EXIF viewer
- 
- If EXIF is completely stripped or looks unnatural, it’s suspicious.
- 
- ---
- 
- ### **2. Look for “generator fingerprints”**
- 
- AI models accidentally leave subtle patterns:
- 
- * Uniform noise patterns
- * Checkerboard textures
- * Repeating micro-artifacts
- * Halos around edges
- 
- Tools that detect this:
- 
- * **Hive Moderation AI Detector**
- * **Illuminarty.ai**
- * **AI or Not**
- * **GPTZero Vision Detector**
- 
- These don’t rely on visible errors but on hidden pixel patterns.
- 
- ---
- 
- ### **3. Analyze lighting consistency**
- 
- Even good AI models struggle with:
- 
- * Shadows falling in wrong directions
- * Light sources not matching across the scene
- * Overly smooth gradients
- 
- Algorithms can detect inconsistencies better than the human eye.
- 
- ---
- 
- ### **4. Error detection models (for hands, eyes, symmetry)**
- 
- You can run images through:
- 
- * **DeepFaceLab detectors**
- * **FaceForensics**
- * **OpenAI’s image authentication tools (limited release)**
- 
- They check:
- 
- * Eye reflections
- * Lens refractions
- * Anatomical alignment
- * Finger count consistency
- 
- This isn’t “looking at it” yourself. These tools analyse mathematically.
- 
- ---
- 
- ### **5. Compression signature analysis**
- 
- AI images often have:
- 
- * Uniform JPEG compression
- * No camera sensor noise
- * No Bayer pattern
- 
- Real cameras create:
- 
- * Unique sensor noise
- * Microscratches
- * Chromatic aberration
- 
- Programs like:
- 
- * **FotoForensics (ELA analysis)**
- * **Forensically.org**
- 
- …can tell the difference by analyzing how the image was compressed.
- 
- ---
- 
- ### **6. Frequency domain analysis**
- 
- This is more advanced.
- 
- Real images have natural frequency curves.
- AI images often show:
- 
- * Repeating frequency spikes
- * Synthetic textures
- 
- Some tools that do this:
- 
- * Matlab scripts
- * Forensic-focused Python libraries
- * NSA/NIST tools used in digital forensics
- 
- This method doesn’t require “looking” at the image in a human way.
- 
- ---
- 
- ### **7. Watermark or hash-based detection**
- 
- Some image generators now embed:
- 
- * **Invisible watermarks**
- * **Cryptographic hashes**
- 
- Tools like:
- 
- * **C2PA Verify**
- * **Content Authenticity Initiative validator**
- 
- These tell you:
- 
- * Who created the image
- * Whether it was altered
- * If it came from an AI generator
-   (If the system was used)
- 
- ---
- 
- ### **8. Cross-checking with reverse image search**
- 
- AI images often appear:
- 
- * In multiple variations online
- * With no original source
- * On prompt websites
- 
- Try:
- 
- * Google Lens
- * Bing Visual Search
- * TinEye
- 
- If the “original source” is a prompt, that’s a giveaway.
- 
- ---
- 
- ## Quick summary
- 
- Other than “visually inspecting,” you can:
- 
- 1. Check EXIF metadata
- 2. Use AI-detection tools
- 3. Analyze lighting consistency
- 4. Use face/hand forensic detectors
- 5. Examine compression patterns
- 6. Look at frequency-domain signatures
- 7. Verify cryptographic watermarks
- 8. Use reverse image search
- 
- None is perfect, but combining them gives you a strong answer.
